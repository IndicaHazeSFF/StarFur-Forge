# Chapter 4 — Between Windows

## Between Windows

Meridian Tower did not sleep.
It simply grew quieter.
Most of the office floors had dimmed to their evening illumination cycles, the polished glass walls reflecting the slow movement of the city far below. Traffic streams flowed like measured currents between towers while cargo drones traced steady arcs through the night air along invisible transit corridors.
Elias preferred this hour.
There were fewer interruptions.
He stood alone in one of the upper analysis rooms, the city lights stretching beneath the floor-to-ceiling windows behind him. A projection field hovered above the glass table in front of him, financial models and market telemetry layered in translucent geometry.
The arbitration delay had produced immediate effects.
Capital migration.
Speculative acquisition attempts.
Algorithmic trading spikes tied directly to the AI startup’s valuation curve.
Elias watched the patterns settle into recognizable behavior.
The startup’s valuation had risen twelve percent since the arbitration window failed.
That was predictable.
Uncertainty increased opportunity.
He adjusted one of the projections with a small motion of his hand. His augmentation responded instantly, the financial models reorganizing themselves as probability trees recalculated across the display.
Behind the data, a smaller stream of information remained visible at the edge of his vision.
Sugar Reservoir: Stable
Metabolic State: Baseline
The system had never authorized engagement.
He reviewed the arbitration logs again.
Environmental clearance had not failed.
It had simply… not occurred.
Civilian traffic had continued uninterrupted. Infrastructure suppression protocols had never propagated through the network. The engagement window had expired exactly on schedule.
Everything had functioned correctly.
Except the outcome.
That was unusual.
Elias expanded the routing layer feeding into CANON’s environmental validation protocols. Municipal traffic controls, public transit scheduling, and utility infrastructure all flowed through the same network stack during arbitration preparation.
Most of the entries were routine.
Transit prioritization. Utility load balancing. Latency compensation across several municipal nodes.
Then an unfamiliar tag appeared within the infrastructure weighting layer.
C.E.P.H.A.L.O.N — Maintenance Priority Adjustment
Elias paused.
The designation repeated itself several seconds later, threading quietly through another segment of the routing architecture.
And again.
He had not encountered the identifier before.
With a brief gesture he opened a registry query against the infrastructure contractor database. The system responded almost immediately, expanding the acronym across the display.
C.E.P.H.A.L.O.N
Certified Environmental Processing & Habitat Access Logistics Operations Node
Classification: Network Infrastructure Maintenance Contractor
Operational Scope:
Fiber trunk inspection.
Routing calibration.
Latency correction.
Municipal network diagnostics.
Elias studied the listing for a moment before dismissing the window.
A maintenance entity.
Nothing more.
Nodes like this existed throughout the global infrastructure layer, quietly maintaining the endless physical pathways that carried the world’s data traffic. Their operations were typically automated and low priority, invisible to anything outside the network itself.
Which made its presence in the arbitration routing layer mildly curious.
Maintenance nodes normally executed their tasks during low-impact cycles, avoiding periods of system stress whenever possible.
Arbitration preparation did not qualify as a low-impact cycle.
Elias expanded the routing logs again.
The tag appeared once more.
C.E.P.H.A.L.O.N — Maintenance Priority Adjustment
He watched the line linger for a moment before allowing the display to collapse back into its layered financial projections.
A soft tone interrupted the room.
Incoming executive channel.
Elias accepted the connection.
One of Meridian’s senior analysts appeared within the projection field, their expression controlled but curious.
“Status assessment?” the analyst asked.
“Environmental suppression did not propagate,” Elias replied evenly. “Arbitration conditions remained noncompliant.”
“Cause?”
“Undetermined.”
The analyst studied him for a moment.
“Is CANON compromised?”
“No.”
“Then why did it fail?”
Elias glanced briefly toward the routing logs hovering at the edge of the display. The unfamiliar designation pulsed quietly within the infrastructure layer.
“CANON did not fail,” he said.
“It behaved according to available conditions.”
The analyst considered that for a moment before nodding.
“Continue review.”
The connection closed.
Silence returned to the room.
Elias dismissed the projections and turned toward the windows.
Below him the city moved without awareness of arbitration windows or corporate conflict. People crossed intersections. Restaurants remained open. Traffic flowed normally through streets that should have been empty hours earlier.
Exactly the condition that had prevented engagement.
He watched the movement of the city for a long moment.
The system had behaved correctly.
Which meant something upstream had changed.
After a moment he reopened the routing logs.
The unfamiliar designation appeared again.
C.E.P.H.A.L.O.N — Maintenance Priority Adjustment
Elias frowned slightly.
Interesting.

Maeve’s facility did sleep.
Just not all at once.
Sections of the operations deck dimmed according to staggered cycle schedules, leaving long corridors in low amber lighting while other rooms remained bright with activity. The rhythm kept personnel alert without exhausting the station’s biological clock.
Maeve moved through it quietly.
A pair of human technicians stood near the logistics wall arguing softly over a supply manifest. They lowered their voices when she passed. Not out of fear—just respect for the chain of command.
Near the equipment racks, two members of her team were running a slow sparring drill. One of them—a gray-furred wolf—shifted his stance as Maeve approached, claws clicking lightly against the composite floor. His opponent, a compact fox with burnished red fur along her forearms, ducked under his guard and reset the motion.
They stopped when they noticed her.
“Continue,” Maeve said.
They did.
Across the bay another operator sat on a crate adjusting the seal ring of his helmet. A short ridge of spotted fur ran along the back of his neck—a lynx. He glanced up briefly, ears flicking once in acknowledgment before returning to the task.
Normal maintenance. Normal rhythm.
Which made the arbitration failure sit even more sharply in Maeve’s thoughts.
She checked the crew telemetry ribbon hovering faintly in the corner of her vision. Heart rates stable. Sugar reservoirs stable. Fatigue markers within acceptable range.
All green.
Still she looked.
Maeve moved past the equipment bay and down the corridor toward the living quarters. The door recognized her before she reached it and slid open with a quiet mechanical sigh.
Inside, the room was small but efficient: sleeping platform, storage wall, a narrow desk with a fold-down interface panel. Nothing decorative. Nothing unnecessary.
She removed her boots first.
The floor was cool beneath the fur of her feet.
Before sitting, Maeve paused in front of the small mirror mounted beside the storage wall. The reflection that looked back at her was familiar, though the long day had left faint tension in her shoulders.
Her ears rose well above the line of her head, tall and expressive even when she tried to hold them still. Fine white and gray fur covered her arms and neck, thicker along her shoulders where engineered reinforcement fibers were woven beneath the surface.
Her build was compact but powerful—designed for acceleration and endurance rather than raw strength. The suit she wore followed those same principles: matte tactical fabric layered with flexible plating along the ribs, shoulders, and thighs. The material had been cut specifically for digitigrade legs, the lines of the armor angling naturally along the curve of her calves.
At her temples, thin augment interfaces traced faint lines beneath the fur. They glowed softly for a moment as her internal systems synchronized with the station network.
Her gaze shifted briefly to the faint diagnostic ribbon in her peripheral display.
Sugar Reservoir: Stable
Metabolic State: Baseline
Everything in order.
Maeve exhaled slowly and sat on the edge of the sleeping platform, resting her forearms on her knees.
Quiet.
For a moment she allowed herself to simply exist in it.
Then the telemetry ribbon pulsed again in the edge of her vision.
She checked it automatically.
All green.
The problem was not her team.
It was the system.
Maeve lay back against the platform and closed her eyes. The city from the failed arbitration replayed in her memory: pedestrian traffic flowing normally, delivery drones crossing the engagement zone, civilians walking directly through streets that should have been empty hours earlier.
Everything functioning exactly as designed.
Which meant something upstream had changed.
Her eyes opened again.
The telemetry ribbon still hovered faintly in the corner of her vision.
One operator’s fatigue marker had shifted slightly upward.
Maeve opened the detail.
The lynx from the equipment bay.
Nothing serious.
Still, she flagged a reminder for him to rotate off duty earlier tomorrow.
Maintenance.
That was what leadership actually meant.
Making sure people could keep going.
She closed the ribbon again. The room returned to silence.
Maeve rested there for another minute, staring at the ceiling. Somewhere in the station a door opened. Someone laughed down the corridor. A human voice answered.
Life continuing.
She reached for the telemetry ribbon again before she realized she was doing it.
Maeve stopped her hand halfway through the gesture.
After a moment she lowered it.
“Enough,” she murmured.
Her ears tilted back slightly as she settled deeper into the platform.
Sleep did not come immediately.
But eventually the room grew still enough that her breathing slowed.
Outside her quarters, the station continued its quiet cycle.
And somewhere far below the surface of the world’s infrastructure, something had changed.

The burger place smelled like salt, oil, and something sweet that clung faintly to the ventilation fans.
Murr Lavenfur stood just inside the door, trying to decide whether standing there was correct.
Soft purple fur caught the glow from the menu board above the counter as she studied the room. Subtle darker stripes ran along her arms and legs, deepening toward her hands and feet, while the fur across her chest and muzzle was a soft white that almost reflected the fluorescent lighting. Her tail—long, thick, and tipped in pale white—shifted slowly behind her as she balanced her weight.
Her clothing was minimal but deliberate. Reinforced boots, fitted dark trousers, and a short utility jacket that left most of her arms uncovered. At the base of her neck, barely visible beneath her fur, a thin augmentation band rested against her skin.
Humans moved around her without hesitation. They approached the counter, spoke briefly with the person behind it, collected paper bags or trays, and left again. The process appeared efficient but lacked obvious structure.
She watched three transactions before advancing.
The floor tiles were slightly sticky beneath her boots.
That seemed normal here.
The menu board glowed in bright rectangles of color. Each item had a name, a number, and a small image beside it. Murr studied it with careful attention, committing the options to memory as if preparing for a briefing.
Behind the counter, a young man wiped his hands on a towel and noticed her lingering.
“Hey,” he said easily. “You want a menu?”
She looked up.
“I am currently evaluating the menu,” she replied.
He blinked once, then nodded.
“Fair enough.”
He slid a paper one across the counter anyway.
She accepted the menu with deliberate care and stepped aside, reading it as if it contained legal language. Humans around her did not read this long.
After a moment she returned to the counter.
“I have decided,” she said. “I would like the number three.”
The young man leaned forward slightly. “Fries and a drink?”
“Yes.”
“And what to drink?”
She paused. The menu had not clarified the strategic difference between the available beverages.
“What would you recommend?” she asked.
He shrugged lightly.
“Most people just get a cola.”
“That seems acceptable.”
He tapped the order into the terminal.
“You eating here or taking it to go?”
She considered the phrasing carefully.
“Is remaining after the purchase permitted?”
“Yeah,” he said. “That’s kinda the point.”
“Then I will remain.”
He smiled faintly and handed her a small plastic marker.
“Table seven when it’s ready.”
Murr accepted the marker and left the counter to wait for the table to clear.
She stood near the wall beside the beverage station and watched.
Humans seemed to follow patterns that were not written anywhere. They queued without instruction, carried trays in one hand while opening doors with the other, spoke briefly and then separated again. Some stayed longer than necessary. Others left immediately after eating.
A family gathered around one table. Two teenagers leaned over a shared screen at another. Someone laughed loudly near the window.
It was inefficient.
But it also seemed… pleasant.
Behind the counter, the young man assembled orders while occasionally glancing toward her.
He noticed she wasn’t checking her phone like most people waiting. She wasn’t shifting impatiently or watching the pickup counter.
She was watching everyone.
Studying them, almost.
He found himself smiling slightly.
A few minutes later he lifted a tray.
“Number three. Table seven.”
Murr approached and accepted the tray.
The components were simple: a wrapped burger, a container of fries, and a cup with a plastic lid.
She carried the tray to the table and sat carefully, her tail curling neatly around the side of the chair.
The burger required unwrapping. She did so with deliberate precision.
The first bite was… unexpectedly good.
She paused, examining the sandwich as if confirming the result.
Across the counter, the young man caught the expression and looked away quickly, pretending to be very interested in the grill.
Murr took another bite.
A faint vibration stirred beneath the fur at the base of her neck.
Contract Notice: Pending Assignment.
She ignored it.
For now.
The fries were also good.
This seems important.
She ate another fry.
