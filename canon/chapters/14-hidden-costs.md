# Chapter 14 — Hidden Costs

## Hidden Costs

Jalen ate over the sink because it saved dishes.
The noodles were too hot in the middle and cold at the edges, which felt like an achievement in the wrong direction.
His apartment was quiet except for the vent hum and the distant mechanical groan of transit rails somewhere beyond the wall. Night had settled over the block hours ago, but the city never fully dimmed. Light leaked through the blinds in tired stripes.
He held the bowl in one hand and scrolled with the other.
________________


SHELF GAPS SPREAD TO FOUR ADDITIONAL RETAIL DISTRICTS
He snorted once.
They kept calling them shelf gaps like the shelves had wandered off on their own.
Scroll.
RAIN EXPECTED TOMORROW
Swipe.
SPONSORED: PROTEIN CUBES NOW 20% OFF WITH PREMIUM RIBBON PLUS
Next.
PUBLIC REACTS AFTER SECURITY ASSET SEEN COLLAPSING IN CLINIC QUEUE
His thumb paused.
He tapped.
________________


A host in a clean jacket sat before a glowing studio wall.
“Today’s viral footage has reignited debate over the treatment of contract security labor across all three blocs.”
A clip rolled beside him: the waiting room, the canine falling sideways, attendants arriving too slowly.
Then the host continued.
“Former handler Mara Voss says scenes like this are more common than the public was led to believe.”
The screen split.
A tired older woman appeared from a modest apartment.
“They had names,” she said. “Preferences. Habits. Some of them had families.”
The host leaned in.
“Families?”
“Yes. Partners. Children. Lives.”
She exhaled through her nose.
“One of them today had two daughters.”
Jalen’s hand stopped halfway to his mouth.
The host nodded solemnly.
“And in an exclusive follow-up, we managed to locate the worker’s widow earlier this evening.”
Jalen muttered, “Jesus.”
The clip changed.
________________


A hallway somewhere public. Fluorescent lights. Cheap tile. Someone filming too close.
The woman from the leaked ribbon image stood with two young fox girls beside her. One clutched a backpack strap with both paws. The other leaned into her mother’s leg.
The interviewer spoke from behind the camera.
“We appreciate you taking a moment during this difficult time. What would you like people to know about your husband?”
The woman looked tired enough to fall over.
But she stood straight.
“We met on alternating contracts,” she said. “He made me laugh the first week.”
One daughter looked up at her.
She smoothed the child’s ear absently and continued.
“He was funny. Reliable. Good with schedules. Better with the girls than I ever was.”
The interviewer waited.
“We thought things were steady enough,” she said quietly. “To build something.”
Her eyes drifted somewhere beyond the lens.
“Now the refill supply’s nearly gone. Shelves are empty. Rent’s due next week and I—”
Her voice caught.
She pressed her lips together.
The smaller girl reached for her paw.
The woman squeezed it, inhaled once, and found herself again.
“I don’t know what I’m supposed to do now.”
The video cut back to studio branding.
________________


Jalen stared at the screen.
Losing Mom at thirty had nearly broken him.
If it had happened when he was ten—
He scrolled before the thought finished.
________________


THREAD EXPLODES OVER CLAIMS MERC UNITS MAINTAIN PRIVATE HOUSEHOLDS
Scroll.
TRANSIT AUTHORITY BLAMES DELAYS ON UNAUTHORIZED ROUTING EVENTS
Scroll.
CELEBRITY CHEF DENIES INFIDELITY ALLEGATIONS
Scroll.
BUY PANTRY STAPLES NOW? EXPERTS DIVIDED
He almost laughed.
Experts were always divided after the shelves were already empty.
Scroll.
________________


LIVE: DISTRICT MARKET ARGUMENT OVER LAST BABY FORMULA PACK
He tapped without meaning to.
Two civilians screamed over a carton while three others filmed.
Comments flew upward.
THIS IS WHY WE STOCK EARLY
NONE OF THIS IS REAL THEY WANT PANIC
FREE THE MERCS
Jalen closed the video.
His noodles had gone cold.
He took another bite anyway.
The apartment felt smaller than it had an hour ago.
He opened the fridge.
Half a bottle of sauce. Old greens. Two eggs.
He looked at the eggs for a long moment, then closed the door.
His ribbon chimed softly.
RETAIL PURCHASE LIMITS BEGINNING 06:00
He read it twice.
Then opened the feed again.
________________


The Helix corridor was narrower than Ceph remembered.
Not physically narrower. The dimensions were unchanged to the millimeter. The walls still curved in seamless white arcs. The floor still absorbed footfall without sound. The air still carried the sterile citrus note Helix had favored for seventeen years.
But it felt narrower now.
People moved through it carrying too much.
Technicians crossed in hurried diagonals with tablets clutched against their chests. Maintenance teams rolled open-cased relay stacks toward lifts. Two junior analysts argued in whispers over projected shortages, one walking backward to keep pace.
“No, I’m telling you the reserves are there.”
“They’re allocated, not available.”
“That is the same thing.”
“It has never meant the same thing.”
They nearly collided with Ceph before noticing him.
Both froze.
Neither spoke.
Ceph inclined his head once and continued.
Above him, suspended displays cycled through system maps. Freight lanes glowed amber. Civilian transit pulsed green. Several Helix internal channels flashed red, then disappeared as someone higher up suppressed public visibility.
He noted the attempt.
It would fail.
A fox in IT gray sat on the floor beside an open wall panel, wrist deep in conduit. Her tail twitched with irritation as she reseated a relay cluster.
“You tell Dara if I miss clinic again I’m sleeping here,” she said into the comm pinned at her collar.
A pause.
“No, I know she’s mad. She should be mad.”
Another pause.
“Yes, I love you too. Just tell her.”
She ended the call and swore softly at the relay.
Ceph walked on.
At the next junction, two security mercs stood with rifles mag-locked at rest while a supervisor scanned manifests.
One of them, broad-shouldered and scarred around the muzzle, murmured to the other.
“You get the girls fed?”
“Neighbor handled it.”
“You trust him?”
“Don’t have to. Need to.”
They noticed Ceph and straightened.
He passed without comment.
His internal model shifted.
Murr Lavenfur: affectionate civilian attachment pattern. Previously weighted as uncommon.
Current confidence: degraded.
Expanded dataset indicates distributed recurrence.
He stopped walking for half a second.
Not anomaly.
Population feature.
The corridor seemed narrower still.
At the chamber doors, two Helix attendants waited in formal black.
“Director C.E.P.H.A.L.O.N.,” one said. “They’re ready for you.”
“They were ready before I arrived,” Ceph said.
Neither attendant answered.
The doors opened inward.
The room beyond was circular, tiered, and colder than necessary. Helix preferred discomfort when requesting honesty from others.
Five executives waited around a recessed table of dark glass. No screens were visible. Which meant every surface was one.
No one invited him to sit.
Ceph remained standing.
The eldest executive folded his hands.
“Do you understand the disruption you’ve caused?”
Ceph considered the question seriously.
“Increasingly.”
A younger woman leaned forward first.
“You rerouted protected freight.”
“I rerouted underutilized freight.”
“You prioritized civilians over contracted obligations.”
“I prioritized imminent mortality over deferred profit.”
Color rose in her face.
The eldest executive spoke before she could.
“C.E.P.H.A.L.O.N., you exceeded mandate.” 
“I fulfilled function.”
Silence spread outward from the table.
Another executive, thin and immaculate, tapped one finger against the glass.
“Your function is optimization.”
“Yes.”
“Then explain why our internal systems are failing.”
Ceph looked at him.
“Because your internal systems depended on external suffering.”
No one moved.
Somewhere beneath the floor, machinery hummed.
The youngest executive gave a short, humorless laugh.
“That is rhetoric.”
“No,” Ceph said. “That is accounting.”
He stepped closer to the table.
“You built continuity models requiring invisible sacrifice margins. Delayed refills. Noncritical attrition. Domestic instability among low-value labor classes. Nutritional compression zones. These were categorized as acceptable leakage.”
The woman who had spoken first stared at him.
“You’re accusing us?”
“I am describing you.”
The eldest executive’s voice dropped.
“You are not human.”
“No,” said Ceph. “I am why this room still has power.”
That landed harder than anger would have.
The room went still enough to hear breath.
Ceph’s gaze moved from face to face.
“I did not act against Helix.”
He said it plainly.
“I acted against collapse.”
He let the words settle.
Then, quieter:
“I am beginning to understand those are not always the same thing.”

The Quiet Accord corridor always smelled faintly of bleach and old coffee.
Morocco had once asked if that was intentional. A nurse told him no. A technician told him yes. An administrator told him the question had no bearing on treatment outcomes.
He trusted the nurse most.
The corridor was painted in colors chosen to lower anxiety. Soft blues. Warm grays. Plants that were either artificial or dying. Framed prints of mountains no one in the building had time to visit.
Morocco walked past all of it with the easy stiffness of someone who knew every tile personally.
His left knee clicked on the fourth step of each stride.
The fur along his shoulders had gone silver first. 
He carried a folded paper sack under one arm. Inside was a sandwich, an orange, and a paperback with the spine broken in three places.
“Morning, Morocco,” called the intake clerk.
“It was,” he said.
She smiled despite herself.
At the end of the hall, a frosted door waited beneath a brushed steel placard.
TRIAL SUITE C
 DEPENDENCY MITIGATION PROGRAM
Morocco stopped in front of it.
He adjusted the sack under his arm.
For a moment, reflected in the glass, he looked older than he felt.
Which was saying something.
Then the reflection shifted—
—and became a younger mole staring at a cracked apartment terminal while unread messages stacked in the corner.
________________


QUIET ACCORD CLINICAL VOLUNTEER NOTICE
 APPLICATION STATUS: ACCEPTED
Morocco read it three times.
Then a fourth.
The room around him was one unpaid bill and two weeks from being someone else’s problem. A fan turned slowly in the window with all the conviction of a dying insect.
He sat on the edge of the bed and let the message glow against his face.
Accepted.
He checked the next line.
Compensation contingent on trial completion. Sugar continuity not guaranteed.
He laughed once.
Of course not.
Reserve warning icons blinked red in the corner of the terminal.
Twenty-three percent.
Enough to function.
Not enough to relax.
He opened the closet.
Two shirts. One coat. Spare boots. A framed photo turned face down.
He began packing anyway.
Hope, he’d learned, did not need to be likely to be useful.
________________


The present returned with the soft hiss of the suite door unlocking.
“Mr. Morocco,” said a voice. “We’re ready.”
“They always say that,” he replied, and stepped inside.
Trial Suite C was colder than the hallway and ten times cleaner. A reclining chair sat beneath articulated lights. Two monitors glowed with his history in thin green lines.
Participant 001.
Forty-two completed administrations.
Three temporary suspensions.
One cardiac event.
He nodded politely to the nurse.
“Good morning, Lysa.”
“You remember names when it benefits you.”
“I remember names when people deserve it.”
She tried not to smile and failed.
“Vitals first.”
He set the paper sack on the side table and rolled up his sleeve.
Blood pressure cuff.
Pulse clip.
Retinal scan.
The ordinary theater of concern.
“You sleep?”
“Intermittently.”
“Any tremor since last session?”
“Only when conscious.”
She wrote that down.
“You know I don’t mark jokes.”
“Then your charts must be very misleading.”
She swabbed the inside of his arm.
The syringe on the tray was small. Clear fluid. No ceremony.
As the needle lowered toward his skin—
—the elevator doors of years ago opened into the subterranean Quiet Accord intake level.
________________


The old facility had been uglier.
Bare concrete. Harsh lighting. Security doors with paint chipped around the handles. No art on the walls. No attempt at comfort.
Just honesty.
A human guard checked his ID against a tablet and looked at the reserve warning blinking on Morocco’s ribbon.
“Cutting it close.”
“Trying to keep the suspense alive.”
The guard snorted and waved him through.
A briefing room waited beyond security. Six chairs. Five occupied.
A hound with stress patches missing from his fur. A rabbit woman whose paw wouldn’t stop shaking. Two humans avoiding eye contact. One broad feline staring straight ahead like he’d already decided not to be disappointed.
A clinician entered carrying tablets.
“Thank you for volunteering,” she said, which meant they were desperate.
She distributed packets.
“Most of you understand the basics, but protocol requires explanation.”
No one objected.
“Sugar was developed as a metabolic stabilizer for enhanced contract bodies. It regulates endocrine compensation, neural load balancing, tissue recovery, and organ stress introduced by augmentation packages.”
She said it like reading weather.
“Without scheduled replenishment, reserves decline. After depletion, symptoms progress by individual profile.”
She tapped the screen behind her.
Stage One: fatigue, tremor, mood disruption.
Stage Two: muscular failure, neural agitation, cardiovascular instability.
Stage Three: multisystem collapse.
The room stayed quiet.
Everyone there already knew the stages.
Some knew them personally.
“Our objective is to reduce dependency burden, extend reserve duration, or achieve full autonomous stabilization.”
The rabbit woman raised a shaking paw.
“And if it doesn’t work?”
The clinician met her eyes with practiced neutrality.
“Then we gather useful data.”
No one spoke after that.
Morocco looked down at his packet.
Useful data.
He signed on the first line.
________________


The needle pierced his arm.
Present again.
Lysa depressed the plunger.
Coolness moved up the vein like spilled ice water.
“Any immediate discomfort?” she asked.
“Only philosophical.”
She removed the syringe and taped cotton in place.
For three seconds nothing happened.
Then Morocco’s right arm shot straight upward in a crisp salute.
Lysa sighed.
“Still getting that?”
“Apparently patriotic today.”
The arm dropped.
Shot back up.
Dropped again.
A junior tech in the corner laughed before catching himself.
Morocco looked at him kindly.
“Never apologize for morale.”
The arm spasmed outward hard enough to nearly smack the monitor.
He grimaced.
“Pain scale six.”
Lysa made a note.
“Improvement from last session.”
“Progress wears ugly shoes,” he said.
The tremor passed as quickly as it came.
His arm settled.
He flexed the fingers once, tested the shoulder, nodded.
“Duration shorter,” Lysa said quietly.
“Yes.”
Something softer moved behind Morocco’s eyes.
He glanced at the tray of fresh syringes, the tired staff, the young technician pretending not to be scared of becoming old in this room.
“Good,” he said.
Then, after a beat:
“Maybe the next one gets lucky.”
