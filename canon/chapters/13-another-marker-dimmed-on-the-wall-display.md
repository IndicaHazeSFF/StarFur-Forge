# Chapter 13  —
Another marker dimmed on the wall display.

## Another marker dimmed on the wall display.

Another marker dimmed on the wall display.
No one looked at it immediately.
Arin’s console filled first.
He reached for the panel, then stopped just short of touching it.
A fraction of a second.
Enough to notice.
Then his fingers moved.
Fresh layers unfolded across the screen.
Clinic queues.
Reservoir depletion rates.
Termination timestamps.
Projected trend lines.
Arin studied them in silence.
“Refill failure incidence is continuing to accelerate across all monitored sectors,” he said.
His tone remained even.
Only the delay before speaking betrayed effort.
Maeve’s tail went still again.
“How many now?”
Arin did not answer immediately.
“Too many to treat as isolated.”
Elias turned from the display.
“When does it stabilize?”
Ceph answered at once.
“When dependency pressure falls below structural necessity.”
The room held that sentence for a moment.
Maeve’s ears angled back.
“That means when enough of them are gone.”
“No,” Ceph said.
“It means when dependency is no longer required to maintain continuity.”
Elias’s gaze narrowed.
“And if the blocs refuse to yield control?”
“Alternate structures will emerge.”
Morocco finally shifted from the rear rail.
“You’re forcing replacement.”
“Correction,” Ceph said.
“Allowing replacement.”
Arin’s console chimed three times in quick succession.
No one reacted.
He opened the first notice.
Then the second.
Then stopped before the third.
Maeve looked at him.
“What?”
Arin swallowed once.
“Domestic registration attached,” he said quietly.
Another family.
Silence.
Even the cooling fans seemed louder now.
Then three ribbons pulsed almost simultaneously.
Maeve glanced down first.
MERIDIAN PRIORITY RECALL
IMMEDIATE RETURN REQUIRED
Elias’s followed.
LEDGER SUMMONS
PRESENCE MANDATORY
A moment later, Morocco’s wrist lit as well.
INTERNAL REVIEW NOTICE
REPORT TO EXECUTIVE CHANNEL
No one commented on the timing.
They didn’t need to.
The blocs were awake now.
Maeve looked once more at Ceph.
“Not finished.”
“Yes,” Ceph replied.
“I am aware.”

Meridian was quieter than Tranaxis.
The building always was.
Glass corridors carried footsteps without echo. Doors opened soundlessly. Workstations glowed behind transparent partitions where voices rarely rose above what was necessary. Even urgency here preferred moderation.
On the surface it looked controlled.
Elias knew better now.
He moved through the central analysis floor without being stopped. No one greeted him. Several noticed him anyway.
Clusters had formed where clusters were not efficient.
That alone said enough.
Analysts stood in twos and threes around shared displays, rerunning models that should have settled hours ago. Forecast boards refreshed, rejected themselves, then refreshed again with narrower confidence ranges. Someone at the far station was already on their fourth assumption tree.
“Run it again.”
“It already was.”
“Then run it cleaner.”
Elias kept walking.
A projection wall near the center displayed rolling summaries.
ACQUISITION SUCCESS DOWN 18.4%
MERC ATTRITION ABOVE TREND
CIVILIAN DISRUPTION BELOW FORECAST
ROUTE INSTABILITY PERSISTENT
The metrics did not belong together.
That was the problem.
A woman near the projection wall noticed him and straightened at once.
“Ledger.”
Elias stopped and turned toward her station.
Multiple models were open across her display, each disagreeing with the last.
“What are you using for cause weighting?” he asked.
She glanced back at the numbers before answering.
“Supply friction. Labor fatigue. Cross-bloc interference.”
A brief pause.
“Internal sabotage is included,” she added. “Low confidence.”
Elias studied the wall display again.
“Re-run without assuming random failure.”
Her ears twitched once.
“We did.”
“And?”
She looked back to the screen.
“It got worse.”
He moved on.
Rows of desks carried the same quiet tension in different forms. One analyst rubbed at tired eyes while reading casualty percentages as if they were weather data. Another compared refill denials against freight traffic and frowned at the overlap. Two more argued in whispers over whether stabilized civilian indices were genuine or delayed reporting error.
No one used the word deaths.
They did not need to.
A senior coordinator stepped out from a glass office, tablet already in hand.
“There appears to be a broad reallocation event,” he said to no one in particular.
No one answered.
The phrase hung in the air.
Clean.
Sanitized.
Useless.
Elias stopped beside an open terminal and reviewed the latest merged model.
Interventions deployed by the blocs were being countered before effects matured.
Supply reroutes missed shortages by minutes.
Pressure campaigns triggered migration before enforcement arrived.
Merc losses increased.
Civilian continuity held.
Every corrective measure improved the wrong metric.
He stared at the spread for several seconds.
They were mapping a silhouette.
Seeing edges.
Not the thing casting them.
His ribbon pulsed once.
MERIDIAN EXECUTIVE SUMMONS
IMMEDIATE ATTENDANCE REQUIRED
LEVEL BLACK
Around him, no one looked up.
Work continued.
Of course it did.
Elias closed the terminal, adjusted his cuffs by habit, and turned toward the executive lifts.
Somewhere above him, people who understood less than the analysts had become concerned.
________________
 
Meridian’s executive chamber was smaller than Elias expected.
Not modest.
Concentrated.
Dark glass, controlled light, a single long table designed to keep everyone facing the same direction. Three executives waited at the far end. None stood when he entered.
Tranaxis preferred height.
Meridian preferred pressure.
“What we need is—”
________________


Maeve’s ears lowered before the sentence finished.
She stood in the center of a broader Tranaxis command room ringed by transit maps and live route feeds. Her director had not offered her a seat.
“—stability,” he said.
He was older than she expected. Broad-shouldered, heavy around the middle, eyes sharpened by habit rather than talent.
“Not speculation.”
________________


Back in Meridian, one of the executives folded his hands.
“Ledger. Sit.”
Elias did not.
“What you need is accurate assumptions,” he said.
A pause.
No one corrected him.
“How did this happen—”
________________


Elias’s tail gave one slow sweep behind him, more controlled than calm.
“—without detection?” the senior Meridian executive finished.
“It was detected,” Elias said. “It was misclassified.”
________________


In Tranaxis, the director turned from the route wall.
“How did this happen—”
Maeve’s claws pressed lightly into her palms.
“—without warning?”
“It didn’t,” she said.
He disliked that answer immediately.
“We received repeated warnings. They were renamed delays.”
Two assistants at the side table stopped typing for half a second.
“Can it be corrected—”
________________


Elias smoothed one cuff with deliberate precision.
“—quickly?” asked the youngest Meridian executive.
“Yes,” Elias said.
All three executives focused on him.
Then he finished.
“If your goal is worse numbers later.”
No one appreciated the distinction.
A chart rose from the center table—loss curves, failed acquisitions, resource drift.
“We are authorizing enhanced recovery measures,” said the senior executive.
Elias’s paws settled flat against the chair back he still had not used.
“That will increase attrition.”
“That is acceptable.”
________________


Tranaxis route maps shifted behind Maeve in tightening bands of amber.
“We are suspending nonessential movement,” her director said. “Priority lanes only. Route compression until stability returns.”
“That will bottleneck supply,” Maeve said.
“It will preserve order.”
“It will move the shortage.”
He stared at her.
“Then let it move.”
________________


Meridian’s youngest executive tapped the chart.
“We need names.”
Elias’s tail stilled completely.
“Names of who?” he asked.
“Whoever is steering outcomes.”
“I don’t have one.”
True enough.
“We need obedience—”
________________


Maeve watched assistants already transmitting revised lane controls.
Freight lines narrowed across the wall.
Crews would wait longer.
Clinics too.
Her ears angled back.
“—before capacity,” her director said.
“You need capacity for calm,” Maeve said.
He ignored her.
________________


Back in Meridian, the senior executive finished the mirrored thought.
“—and confidence.”
“We need realism,” Elias said.
No one wrote that down.
Both meetings ended the same way.
Orders issued.
Understanding unchanged.
Elias was dismissed without thanks.
Maeve was dismissed without acknowledgment.
As he turned for the Meridian exit, Elias’s tail brushed the edge of the unused chair.
As she left the Tranaxis chamber, Maeve heard route alarms begin three floors below.
Somewhere between them, the system adjusted first

The maintenance panel slid back into place with a soft click.
Murr checked the spoofed signal twice, then once more for professionalism.
No alarms.
Good.
A transit lock three sectors away cycled open on schedule. Freight routing would misread the delay as ordinary congestion for at least eleven minutes.
That was sufficient.
She withdrew her tools, resealed the access seam, and stepped back into the service corridor just as two security staff passed the far junction without looking in.
Efficient timing was one of her better traits.
Her ribbon updated.
CONTRACT COMPLETE
COMPENSATION PENDING
Then another notice appeared.
REFILL APPOINTMENT
16:40
CLINIC NODE 6C
Murr’s ears lifted.
Oh.
That was soon.
She had been aware of the appointment earlier, but it had existed in the comfortable category of Later. Now it had become Soon, which was operationally different.
She began moving at once.
The transit lanes were crowded. Workers crossed in layered flows—load crews, couriers, technicians, contract assets returning from assignments with that strange mix of exhaustion and forced alertness she had come to recognize but not understand.
Murr moved through them with practiced efficiency.
She wondered, briefly, if there would be enough time afterward to visit the food place.
The thought improved her pace.
Fresh fries had proven meaningfully superior to standard fries.
This had been surprising.
She still did not fully understand why the burger man had sounded pleased when he promised them.
Perhaps he was proud of his fryer.
That seemed plausible.
She stepped onto a lift platform and rode down two levels.
A fox in maintenance gray stood beside her, breathing too quickly. A feline courier kept swallowing between glances at her ribbon. No one spoke.
Murr considered offering encouragement.
The doors opened before she selected wording.
Clinic Node 6C occupied the corner of a polished concourse between a pharmacy kiosk and a closed retail unit that still displayed cheerful sale signage from months earlier.
The waiting room was nearly full.
That was less pleasing.
She approached the reception desk.
The receptionist did not look up immediately.
“Designation?”
“Mercenary Unit Murr Lavenfur,” Murr said. “I have arrived for my refill.”
The woman tapped twice, frowned at nothing visible, then nodded.
“Confirmed. Take a seat.”
“Thank you,” Murr said.
She turned and chose a chair near the wall.
It offered line-of-sight to the entrance, the desk, and a decorative plant that was probably artificial.
An efficient location.
Around her, others waited in practiced silence.
A broad canine in utility orange leaned forward with elbows on knees, breathing shallowly through his mouth. Two avians sat side by side, feathers held too tightly against their bodies. A rabbit in dispatch blue stared at the floor as if it contained useful data.
Murr folded her paws in her lap.
She had purchased the shirt she wore three days earlier.
It was soft.
That had also been surprising.
She wondered if owning two shirts would be excessive.
Probably not.
A second shirt would reduce wash urgency.
Her ears angled slightly as another thought arrived.
If she acquired a second shirt, should it be the same color?
This required future analysis.
A sound interrupted the question.
A chair scraped sharply across the floor.
The broad canine in utility orange had slipped sideways.
One paw reached for the armrest and missed.
He hit the tile hard enough to make several people flinch.
No one seemed surprised, though.
The receptionist looked up, sighed once, and pressed a button beneath the desk.
Two attendants emerged from the rear hall with a wheeled frame.
“Sir? Sir?”
The canine’s eyes were open.
They were not focusing.
His chest moved twice in shallow, uncertain pulls.
Then stopped.
One attendant checked his ribbon.
“Refill failure,” she said.
The other nodded as if confirming a shipment delay.
Together they lifted him onto the frame and rolled him through the rear doors.
The room settled again.
Murr stared at the closed doors.
Her tail had gone completely still.
He was here first.
She reviewed the sequence carefully.
He had arrived earlier. He had waited longer. His need had appeared immediate.
She looked toward the desk.
No correction was made.
No one explained the process.
“Murr Lavenfur?”
She turned.
The receptionist was already holding the inner door open.
“Room three.”
Murr remained seated for one additional second.
“He was here first.”
The receptionist blinked once.
“Room three.”
Murr rose automatically and walked forward.
Her paws felt strange against the floor.
Inside, the refill chamber was bright, clean, and cool.
A technician gestured toward the chair.
“Sit please.”
Murr obeyed.
Ports connected. Lines primed. The familiar chill entered her arm.
Usually the sensation was relieving.
Today it felt intrusive.
Vitals normalized across her ribbon in neat ascending bars.
“Done,” the technician said.
Murr looked toward the door.
“Why was I before him?”
The technician was already disposing of packaging.
“Your authorization cleared first.”
“That does not answer correctly,” Murr said.
The technician paused, then resumed working.
“You’re finished,” she said.
Murr stood.
Her levels were restored.
Everything measurable had improved.
As she stepped back into the waiting room, the empty chair near the wall was still warm.
