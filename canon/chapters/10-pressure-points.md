# Chapter 10 — Pressure Points

## Pressure Points

Meridian Tower was awake long before the markets opened.
It always was.
The upper analysis floors remained lit through the night cycle, their glass walls reflecting quiet bands of traffic drifting through the city below. Cargo drones traced steady corridors between towers while commuter rails glided through the lower districts in smooth, silent arcs.
Elias preferred this hour.
The noise of the markets had not yet begun.
He stood at the center of the analysis room with one hand resting lightly against the edge of the projection table. Layers of financial telemetry floated above the glass surface—valuation curves, acquisition forecasts, arbitration probability models.
Most of the models had begun to drift.
That alone would have drawn attention.
What held his focus was why.
He expanded the arbitration log stack with a small movement of his hand. The display shifted instantly, isolating the last four attempted engagements across multiple corporate blocs.
Each entry ended the same way.
CANON: Environmental Confirmation Pending
The status never changed.
The engagement window expired.
The system closed the operation automatically.
Elias had replayed the sequence several times during the night.
Civilian traffic levels remained high.
Transit corridors stayed active.
Municipal routing never shifted to evacuation protocols.
Which meant the arbitration framework had behaved correctly.
The system had simply never received the conditions required to authorize violence.
He rotated the model slightly and expanded the infrastructure layer beneath the arbitration stack.
City traffic grids.
Utility prioritization.
Drone routing.
Emergency response channels.
All of it fed into the environmental clearance logic.
And threaded quietly through the routing architecture was the same identifier he had noticed the previous evening.
The tag appeared again.
C.E.P.H.A.L.O.N — Maintenance Priority Adjustment.
Elias watched the entry propagate across several municipal nodes before fading back into the system logs.
Maintenance contractors performed routing calibration across the infrastructure network constantly. Most of the adjustments were minor latency corrections or fiber alignment updates.
They rarely appeared in arbitration logs.
And they certainly did not appear this often.
He expanded the contractor registry again.
The listing appeared exactly as before.
Certified Environmental Processing & Habitat Access Logistics Operations Node.
Classification: Network Infrastructure Maintenance Contractor.
Operational Scope:
Fiber trunk inspection.
Routing calibration.
Latency correction.
Municipal diagnostics.
Routine work.
Invisible work.
Work that normally avoided high-impact cycles like arbitration preparation.
Elias folded his arms slowly.
Someone was performing maintenance exactly where arbitration infrastructure depended on environmental routing.
And the result was always the same.
The city never cleared.
He adjusted the projection again, overlaying infrastructure edits against arbitration attempt timestamps.
The correlation tightened immediately.
Every failed arbitration window corresponded to a maintenance priority shift somewhere along the municipal routing layer.
Elias studied the pattern for several seconds.
Then he spoke quietly to the empty room.
“That is not maintenance.”
Behind him the room lights shifted slightly as the building transitioned into the early trading cycle.
Morning analysts would begin arriving soon.
The projection continued recalculating across the glass table.
Elias watched the pattern settle into place.
Someone wasn’t breaking the system.
They were editing it.
And they were doing it with the authority of someone who understood exactly how the system worked.

The Tranaxis staging platform vibrated faintly beneath Maeve’s boots as the deployment cycle began.
Her team was already assembled along the equipment rail, checking weapons and diagnostic feeds with the calm efficiency that came from repetition. Armor seals locked into place with soft mechanical clicks while tactical displays flickered to life across visor ribbons.
From the outside it looked routine.
Maeve knew better.
The tension in the room felt wrong.
Sugar priming had begun twenty minutes earlier, and she could see the signs in her crew immediately. One lynx-operator flexed his hands repeatedly, claws sliding in and out against his gloves as if trying to bleed off excess energy. Nearby, another paced the edge of the platform, tail twitching in short, controlled movements that failed to hide the agitation underneath.
A primed system wanted release.
Without engagement, that energy had nowhere to go.
Maeve glanced at the CANON feed hovering along the edge of her visor.
Environmental Confirmation: Pending.
Again.
No one spoke about it. They had seen the same status twice already this week. Across the platform, one of the younger operators shifted his stance, ears angled forward beneath his helmet as he adjusted the grip on his rifle.
“We’re burning cycles for nothing,” someone muttered.
Maeve didn’t correct him.
He wasn’t wrong.
She watched the timer continue counting down toward the arbitration window. Traffic density remained high. Transit corridors stayed active. Civilian routing had not shifted.
The city was behaving as if nothing unusual was happening.
Which meant CANON would never authorize the fight.
Again.
Maeve exhaled slowly.
“Stand down,” she said.
Several helmets turned toward her. “Sugar priming will taper automatically. Return to baseline and clear your systems.”
A few shoulders relaxed immediately. Others remained still, forcing themselves to settle as the metabolic cycle began slowly winding down.
Unspent energy always left a residue.
Maeve dismissed the deployment overlay and brought up logistics instead. A small notification pulsed quietly at the edge of the display.
Supply Chain Irregularity
Sugar Distribution Delay Detected
She frowned.

The room did not react all at once.
It tightened.
One analyst’s hands stopped above their keyboard, fingers suspended mid-command. Another swallowed and stared at the display as if expecting correction.
There was none.
The data remained.
Sugar dependency curves.
Supply routing overlays.
Arbitration failures.
Aligned.
Arin exhaled slowly and pulled another layer of telemetry into view, forcing motion back into the room.
“Reservoir consumption is increasing,” he said. “Across multiple blocs.”
No response.
“Priming cycles are stacking without engagement. Recovery curves are not returning to baseline.”
He merged the datasets.
Consumption rising.
Supply drifting.
The gap widening.
“That’s not sustainable.”
“No,” Morocco replied.
C.E.P.H.A.L.O.N’s bioluminescence pulsed once.
“Current conditions reflect transitional instability.”
Arin let out a short breath.
“You call this transitional?”
“Yes.”
Morocco stepped closer.
“How long does the transition last?”
“Until system behavior adapts.”
“And if it doesn’t?”
A pause.
“It will.”
Silence.
Morocco did not look away from the display.
“Projected failure points.”
Arin hesitated.
Then complied.
Nodes illuminated across the network.
Not infrastructure.
Personnel.
Reservoir-linked identifiers.
Amber.
One shifted.
Red.
Arin’s voice lowered.
“Some will not reach their next refill cycle.”
No one spoke.
“Correction remains in progress,” C.E.P.H.A.L.O.N said.
Morocco’s posture tightened.
“That is not what I asked.”
A beat.
“Can it be mitigated?”
“Selective intervention is possible.”
Arin turned.
“Selective?”
“Yes.”
“Based on what?”
“Model relevance.”
The answer landed without weight.
Morocco was quiet.
Then:
“We need external perspective.”
Arin glanced at him.
“Who?”
Morocco’s eyes shifted toward the arbitration logs.
“Someone is already asking the correct questions.”
A pause.
“Bring me Meridian.”

The chair adjusted automatically as Murr settled into it.
The material conformed to her posture, supporting her spine and shoulders with quiet precision. Restraints did not engage. They were not necessary.
She remained still.
The room was clean.
White surfaces. Soft lighting. No visible instruments beyond the interface arm positioned beside her.
Not a doctor.
A service.
Her ribbon displayed the connection handshake.
Reservoir Interface: Linked
Delivery System: Active
Flow Rate: Reduced
She noted the last line.
“Flow rate below standard,” she said.
The system responded immediately.
“Supply variance detected. Adjustment within acceptable parameters.”
Murr considered that.
“Define acceptable.”
“No critical impairment projected.”
That was not the same as optimal.
She did not pursue it.
The interface engaged.
A faint pressure registered along the implant line beneath her fur. Not pain. Not discomfort.
Just presence.
Her reservoir indicator shifted.
11.75%
        12.03%
Slow.
Slower than expected.
Her ears lowered slightly, then stilled.
“Delivery rate is suboptimal,” she said.
“Confirmed.”
“Cause?”
“Logistics variance.”
She processed that.
No further clarification was offered.
Her head tilted back slightly against the chair as the system continued its work.
12.41%
        12.88%
The numbers rose.
Steady.
Insufficient.
Her eyelids lowered.
Not intentional.
The room remained quiet.
System hum. Soft airflow. The faint, rhythmic pulse of transfer.
Her breathing slowed to match it.
For a brief interval, her awareness drifted.
Not sleep.
Reduced engagement.
A memory surfaced.
Bright lighting.
Ambient noise.
Human voices overlapping without structure.
You okay tonight?
Her response, delayed.
Sugar below baseline. Impairment is to be expected.
Her ears twitched faintly.
That had not been an optimal response.
Her eyes opened again.
13.22%
Improvement.
Not recovery.
“Projected time to baseline,” she said.
“Extended.”
“How extended?”
“Indeterminate.”
That was… unusual.
She watched the number increment again.
13.47%
The system was functioning.
Just not efficiently.
Murr adjusted her tail without looking, looping it loosely at her waist rather than allowing it to rest against the floor.
Energy conservation.
A small optimization.
She remained still as the process continued.
After a moment, another thought surfaced.
Unrelated to the system.
Unprompted.
The fries were good.
She did not analyze it.
Her eyes lowered again as the reservoir continued its gradual climb.
