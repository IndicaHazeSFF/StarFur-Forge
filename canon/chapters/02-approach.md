# Chapter 2 — Approach

## Approach

Maeve stood at the front of the briefing room with her hands resting lightly on the edge of the table.
The room was unadorned—steel, composite paneling, recessed lighting set to neutral daylight. No insignia. No motivational displays. Arbitration units did not require inspiration.
A three-dimensional projection hovered above the table surface: a mid-rise commercial structure overlaid with zoning lines and entry vectors.
“Mandate is Seize,” she said. Her voice was steady, unforced. “Primary objective: secure computational core on levels seven through nine. Establish uncontested control for validation interval.”
The projection shifted, highlighting vertical access shafts.
“Secondary is Deny. If seizure cannot be validated within window, isolate and prevent rival integration.”
No one interrupted. No one needed to.
She allowed the final layer to illuminate.
“Tertiary: Nullify. Only if confirmation of compromise exceeds seventy percent and authorization threshold is met.”
That last clause was ritual language. Everyone in the room understood what it meant.
Don’t escalate unless the system says you can.
Maeve glanced around the table, meeting each gaze briefly. Lagomorph ears angled subtly, catching the room’s smallest movements. Her posture remained relaxed but balanced, weight distributed forward as though she might move at any moment.
“Environmental clearance is expected at minus five,” she continued. “Transit taper begins at minus eight. Engagement window opens on CANON authorization.”
A few quiet acknowledgments flickered across internal comms. Her team checked gear without theatrical motion—flexing gloved fingers, calibrating HUD layers, adjusting fit around tail ports and knee articulation. Suits were matte and modular, plates integrated rather than strapped. Designed to move through structures without damaging what didn’t need breaking.
Maeve deactivated the projection.
“Remember the envelope,” she said. “Civilian property remains out-of-bounds. If it isn’t on the warrant, it doesn’t exist.”
One of the younger operators shifted slightly. “Expected resistance level?”
“Moderate,” Maeve replied. “Competing unit staging confirmed. Precision engagement. Minimal duration.”
Her tone carried neither optimism nor warning. Just alignment.
She stepped back from the table and glanced once at the biometric ribbon in her peripheral display.
“Reservoir levels,” she said.
Green indicators pulsed across her team’s feeds.
“Sugar sync confirmed,” one operator replied. “Release threshold on authorization.”
“Good,” Maeve said.
“Questions?”
None.
“Then we move.”
Chairs slid back in near silence. Helmets remained unclasped; there was still time before seal.
As they approached the exit, Maeve’s reflection ghosted briefly in the polished surface of the door. White and grey fur caught the overhead light in muted contrast. Compact frame. Long ears angled slightly back in concentration. Tail low, controlled. Built for acceleration in tight spaces, for threading through collapsing corridors and unstable terrain.
She rested her hand on the door control.
“On window,” she said quietly. “Not before.”
The door segmented open.
The door parted with a quiet seal release.
Beyond it, the corridor curved toward the platform’s deployment spine. Through reinforced viewports, the planet rotated below in slow indifference—cloud bands sliding over ocean, city clusters glinting faintly against dusk.
Maeve’s team moved as a unit toward ingress staging. Atmospheric insertion vectors were already calculated. They would descend into a cleared envelope.
They always did.

On the surface, within a tower owned by the Meridian Compact, Elias Voss dismissed the last predictive overlay from his table.
The same building projection hovered in front of him.
He had already removed three variables from the model.
Elias did not conduct briefings.
He conducted confirmations.
The room assigned to Meridian Compact arbitration teams resembled a conference suite more than a staging area. Glass walls polarized to opacity, muting the skyline beyond. The table surface was an uninterrupted sheet of reactive composite, displaying projections only when addressed. No equipment racks. No visible armor. No wasted presence.
His team stood rather than sat.
A wireframe model of the target structure rotated slowly above the table, annotated with acquisition pathways and probability bands. Elias adjusted one of the overlays with two fingers, narrowing a corridor of projected resistance until it vanished.
“Revised entry vector uploaded,” he said. His voice carried easily without being raised. “We will not require it unless Transaxis delays environmental taper.”
One of the operators nodded once, already integrating the change.
“Mandate remains Seize,” Elias continued. “Nullification carries negative yield. Avoid unless forced.”
He did not explain why. They already understood the economics.
A destroyed asset had no predictive value.
He dismissed another projection—civilian density modeling—without looking at it for more than a second.
“Clearance will remove nonparticipants,” he said. “Do not factor them into timing.”
Unlike Maeve’s team, Meridian operators wore lighter integration layers beneath tailored outer garments. Reinforcement existed, but it was hidden—load-distributing fabrics, embedded response mesh, discreet interface nodes at wrist and collar. They resembled executives arriving early to a negotiation.
Which, in a sense, they were.
One of them spoke. “Engagement window confirmation?”
Elias checked the status feed hovering at the edge of his vision.
CANON: Pending Environmental Compliance.
He paused.
That line should have resolved already.
“It will open,” he said, more to close the question than from certainty. “Prepare for immediate validation sequence.”
No one challenged the statement. CANON delays were statistical noise. The system converged. It always converged.
“Summary refresh,” Elias said.
A compact data column unfolded above the table.
Entity: LatticeMind Cooperative
Classification: Independent Optimization Engine
Status: Non-aligned
Growth Curve: Non-linear. Unlicensed deployment detected in twelve municipal systems.
One of the analysts spoke without looking up. “They’re not selling a product. They’re offering adaptive logistics forecasting as a public utility layer.”
“Yes,” Elias said. “Which means they are not pricing access.”
A faint shift of attention moved through the room. That detail mattered more than any technical specification.
“If left independent,” another operator added, “their model begins replacing contractual routing markets.”
“Not replacing,” Elias corrected. “Obscuring. You cannot arbitrate value against a system that continuously redefines efficiency.”
He expanded the projection. Infrastructure maps bloomed outward—transit, supply, micro-distribution networks—threads of optimization already touching Meridian-managed flows.
“They are teaching cities to optimize themselves without us,” he said. “That is unsustainable.”
“Helix attempted acquisition last quarter,” someone noted.
“Helix wants to study it,” Elias replied. “We require containment.”
He reduced the display back to the single building footprint.
“Which is why this is an arbitration and not a negotiation. Once CANON validates seizure, their engine becomes a licensed instrument. Predictable. Metered. Governable.”
He let that settle.
“Until then, treat it as volatile infrastructure. We are not here to damage it unless forced. We are here to ensure it does not remain ownerless.”
A soft tone indicated another CANON status check.
Still pending.
Elias closed the data column.
“Positions,” he said. “We move the moment authorization resolves.”
Elias stepped away from the table.
“Metabolic modulation linked to window trigger,” he said. A fractional pause. “No premature cycling.”
“Confirmed,” came the response.
“Seal and stage,” he said.
The projection dissolved. The glass walls cleared to transparency, revealing the financial district’s vertical geometry—steel and composite rising in disciplined repetition. Traffic streamed between towers in regulated lanes. Pedestrian flow moved in synchronized pulses below. The city was behaving precisely as forecast.
His team moved without hurry. Meridian deployments did not rush toward conflict. They entered it at the moment probability tipped in their favor.
As they reached the corridor, one operator checked the shared feed.
“Window still pending.”
Elias didn’t slow. “Environmental taper is within acceptable latency.”
A fractional pause.
“It will resolve.”
The corridor opened into a private transit shaft. Their transport waited below—a compact vertical-lift aircraft finished in muted graphite, its surfaces seamless except for embedded sensor arrays and adaptive paneling. It did not resemble a military craft. It resembled a discretionary acquisition.
They boarded in silence.
Elias took the forward standing position as the hatch sealed.
“Engage cloak protocols on approach,” he said. “Full thermal suppression inside municipal airspace. Maintain civilian-safe descent corridor.”
“Cloak on approach,” the pilot confirmed. “Thermal dampening synchronized.”
“Asset roof access?”
“Unlocked via proxy thirty seconds ago. No alerts triggered.”
“Good.”
The aircraft lifted vertically, smooth and controlled. The skyline fell away beneath them as they ascended into regulated air lanes before peeling off toward the arbitration grid.
Inside the cabin, one of his operators spoke again. “Civilian density model remains elevated.”
Elias glanced at the data ribbon scrolling at the edge of his vision.
CANON: Awaiting Environmental Confirmation.
“Clearance directives were issued,” he said. “Infrastructure will comply.”
He believed that. Not as optimism—simply as precedent.

High above the atmosphere, Maeve’s insertion craft rotated along its descent vector.
The planet filled the forward viewport—cloud bands drifting over coastal illumination, the city’s grid faint but distinct. Transaxis vessels were not beautiful. They were built to survive violent transitions between environments.
“Trajectory stable,” the pilot reported.
Maeve stood secured within the cabin, helmet sealed, HUD layers faint across her vision. Her team remained clipped into their restraints. No one spoke unnecessarily. Their breathing was steady inside comms.
Reentry plasma began to flare across the forward hull, a controlled corona of heat that shimmered along the viewport edges.
“Angle holding,” the pilot said. “Retro burn in three.”
Maeve braced.
“Two.”
The burn ignited—an abrupt, crushing deceleration that pressed weight through engineered muscle and reinforced bone. The ship absorbed most of it; the rest transmitted as a reminder that physics still applied.
“One.”
The pressure eased.
“Burn complete. Atmospheric insertion nominal. Cloak engaging.”
Exterior visibility shifted as adaptive panels refracted thermal and optical signatures. To outside observers, nothing descended. No anomaly registered on civilian tracking systems. No unauthorized object breached airspace.
Maeve switched her feed to environmental clearance.
Traffic density: ninety-two percent baseline.
Her eyes narrowed slightly.
“Transit taper?” she asked.
“Negative shift,” an operator replied. “Holding above expected threshold.”
“It’s early,” Maeve said evenly. “Clearance at minus five.”
The craft settled into a shallow depression beyond the municipal perimeter, masked by infrastructure shielding. The ramp lowered without sound.
They stepped into cool night air.
Maeve scanned the horizon once before gesturing toward a recessed maintenance access partially hidden behind industrial shielding.
“Sub-surface ingress,” she said. “Standard route.”
Transaxis doctrine favored controlled geometry over open streets. Sewers, maintenance corridors, and utility spines reduced unpredictability. The team descended.
Concrete walls narrowed around them. Moisture tracked along pipes. Power conduits hummed faintly overhead. The city’s hidden structure felt more honest than its surface—raw function without display.
Maeve checked the countdown in her peripheral display.
Engagement window: minus three.
“Maintain low profile,” she said. “Hold until authorization.”
They ascended through a final vertical shaft and emerged into an access corridor adjacent to the target structure’s lower mechanical levels. From here, a vertical advance would place them directly beneath the computational floors.
Maeve raised a hand.
Hold.
She listened.
Above them, traffic flowed.
Pedestrian footsteps transmitted faint vibration through the structure. A rideshare vehicle idled longer than it should have at the curb. Somewhere nearby, someone laughed.
She checked the feed again.
Civilian density unchanged.
Engagement window: minus one.
Still no authorization tone.
Her ears angled forward slightly beneath the helmet.
“Status?” she asked over encrypted channel.
A beat.
“Window pending.”
That was wrong.

Across the city, Meridian’s aircraft adjusted into a vertical descent vector above the target structure.
“Final approach,” the pilot said. “Cloak at full. Civilian visual suppression holding.”
“Maintain hover offset until authorization,” Elias instructed. “We do not touch the roof surface without CANON confirmation.”
“Understood.”
The aircraft stabilized above the building, invisible against the night.
Inside the cabin, Elias monitored the arbitration channel.
CANON: Awaiting Environmental Confirmation.
He watched the street below through filtered optics.
Traffic lights cycled.
Pedestrians crossed.
The city did not thin.
And the window did not open.
________________


Maeve held position longer than the timer required.
“Thermal shift above us,” one of her operators murmured. “Localized. Rooftop level.”
Not CANON. Not authorization.
Another team.
Maeve already knew that. Arbitration always meant opposition. Still, she keyed her optics upward through structural overlays. A faint distortion—barely measurable—suggested a cloaked platform settling into hover.
Meridian.
One of her people adjusted stance unconsciously, weight shifting forward. Not advancing. Not retreating. The posture of something ready to move if permitted.
Maeve didn’t look at him.
“Hold discipline,” she said.
The words were calm. Absolute.
The operator eased back a fraction.
Above them, unseen but equally aware, Meridian sensors registered the Transaxis presence below.
Inside the aircraft, one of Elias’ analysts spoke. “Secondary unit confirmed in structure. Transaxis insertion successful.”
Elias watched the shared feed without expression.
“Maintain observational posture,” he said. “We do not acknowledge outside CANON parameters.”
A pause.
“They’re waiting too,” the analyst added.
“Of course they are,” he responded.
Below, Maeve listened to the city continue around them—engines, footsteps, a distant siren unrelated to anything here.
Two arbitration teams. Fully deployed.
Legally unable to act.
The tension wasn’t aggression. It was interruption. A hunt paused mid-stride.

In Quiet Accord Operations, the arbitration timeline extended past its expected boundary.
Arin watched the counter advance into territory that normally never appeared on-screen. The interface did not flash warnings. It simply continued measuring time that was not supposed to exist.
Engagement Window: Not Achieved.
Validation State: Pending.
He cross-checked environmental telemetry again. Civilian density remained stable. Transit flowed. Municipal systems reported no compliance anomalies because, from their perspective, nothing abnormal had been requested.
A supervisor’s voice carried from two stations over. “CANON hasn’t declared?”
Arin shook his head without looking away. “It hasn’t opened.”
“That’s not possible.”
Arin didn’t answer. The data wasn’t broken. It was consistent—perfectly, stubbornly consistent.
A new line populated.
ARBITRATION STATUS: DEFERRED — NO VALIDATION STATE.
He stared at it for a moment before routing the notice outward to deployed units.
For the first time since he’d joined Quiet Accord, an arbitration had produced no outcome at all.
________________


Her channel chimed.
Priority override.
She accepted.
“Transaxis Unit Seven,” the control voice said. “Arbitration deferred. Stand down and withdraw to staging perimeter.”
Maeve absorbed that without reacting.
“Reason?” she asked.
“CANON authorization not achieved.”
That was not an answer. It was an admission.
Maeve closed the channel.
“Disengage,” she told her team. “Return to ingress route. Same order we came in.”
No one argued. The energy shifted—not relief, not frustration. Just unused readiness.
________________


Elias received the same transmission seconds later.
“Meridian Unit Three,” Control said. “Arbitration deferred. Hold action and return to origin vector.”
He looked once more at the building below, at the unaltered flow of traffic surrounding it.
“Understood,” he replied.
He did not ask why.
Because there was no precedent to reference if he did.
“Pilot,” he said, “break approach. Resume civilian corridor.”
The aircraft lifted away, still invisible.
Below, the city never realized it had almost hosted a conflict.
And CANON never opened the window.
